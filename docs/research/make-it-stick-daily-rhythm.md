# What *Make It Stick* says about daily rhythm

Ticket: amierlorenz/articulate#3. Feeds PLAN.md §10 (Learning Loop) and §11 (Quiz System).

**Source:** Peter C. Brown, Henry L. Roediger III, Mark A. McDaniel, *Make It Stick: The Science
of Successful Learning* (Belknap/Harvard, 2014). Read from the local PDF
(`mieracleStudyDev/Make It Stick .pdf`). Page numbers below are the **printed book pages**
(PDF page minus 15). Quotes are kept short; this is a copyrighted book.

**How to read this:** each rule is tagged **[book]** when the book states it directly, or
**[inference]** when it is my application of the book to Articulate. The book is about
principles, not schedules: it gives no minute counts for a study session and no formula for
how the mix should change as an exam approaches. Where it is silent, this note says so.

## 1. Before class: generation, not a pretest

The book never uses the word "pretesting". What it offers is **generation**: "an attempt to
answer a question or solve a problem before being shown the answer or the solution" (p. 208).

- **[book]** Attempting first helps even when you are wrong: unsuccessful attempts "encourage
  deep processing of the answer when it is later supplied" (p. 88); "It's better to attempt a
  solution and supply the incorrect answer than not to make the attempt" (p. 88). Same claim
  in the chapter takeaway, with the condition attached: "so long as corrective feedback is
  provided" (p. 101; also p. 91, p. 226).
- **[book]** Concrete student moves before reading or class:
  - Before reading, explain "the key ideas you expect to find in the material and how you
    expect they will relate to your prior knowledge", then read to check (p. 209).
  - In math/science, "try to solve the problems before you get to class" (p. 209; Washington
    University physics requires it). Teachers' version: wrestle with "a new kind of problem
    before coming to the class where the solution is taught" (p. 227).
  - The intro-psych student profile: "Always does the reading prior to a lecture" and
    "Anticipates test questions and their answers as he reads" (p. 216).
- **[book]** The closest thing to a pretest is the Columbia Middle School design: one quiz "at
  the start of class, on material from assigned reading that hadn't yet been discussed"
  (p. 34), kept as "quizzes before lessons, quizzes after lessons, and then a review quiz
  prior to the chapter test" (p. 36). Note it tests *assigned reading*, not totally unseen
  material.
- **[book] Limit:** a difficulty is only desirable if the learner can overcome it. If the
  learner "does not have the background knowledge or skills to respond to them successfully,
  they become undesirable difficulties" (p. 98, quoting the Bjorks); "a difficulty must be
  something learners can overcome through increased effort" (p. 99).

**Rules for Articulate**
1. **[inference]** The pre-class preview should be a short *generation* prompt, not a graded
   quiz: "What do you expect today's lecture on X to cover? How does it connect to Y?" plus,
   for problem courses, one or two problems of the type to be taught.
2. **[inference]** Build it from material the student *can* reason about (syllabus topic,
   assigned reading, prior lectures), not from details only the lecture will reveal, so the
   difficulty stays desirable (p. 98-99).
3. **[book → design]** Preview answers must be closed out with feedback. Link the preview to
   the post-class quiz so the lecture (and the quiz) supply the correction (p. 88, p. 101).
   Do not schedule preview items into SM-2 as cards on their own.

## 2. Right after class: one quick retrieval, then let it rest

- **[book]** Testing right after exposure beats not testing: in one study an immediate test
  raised week-later recall from 28% to 39%; three immediate tests held it at 53% (p. 31-32).
  The Columbia design gave a quiz "at the end of class after the teacher had covered the
  material" (p. 34), and end-of-class testing "increases attentiveness during class" (p. 43).
- **[book]** Real-classroom examples of the same move: McDermott's "four-item quiz in the
  last three to five minutes of every class", drawing on anything covered to date (p. 237);
  Wenderoth's ten minutes of free recall "at the end of each day" on a blank sheet, then
  checking notes and focusing on what was forgotten (p. 231).
- **[book]** Reflection after a lecture: "What are the key ideas? What are some examples? How
  do these relate to what I already know?" (p. 88; also p. 209-210).
- **[book]** But the *next* retrieval should wait: "After an initial test, delaying subsequent
  retrieval practice is more potent ... because delayed retrieval requires more effort"
  (p. 43). Delayed practice beat immediate practice even at a gap of twenty word pairs (p. 32).
- **[book]** Feedback: corrective feedback beats testing alone, and "delaying the feedback
  briefly produces better long-term learning than immediate feedback" (p. 39-40), e.g.
  answering closed-book first, then looking at the source (p. 40).

**Rules for Articulate**
4. **[book]** Post-class quiz = short (a handful of items, a few minutes), closed-book,
   free-recall or short-answer, on the main ideas of that lecture (p. 34, p. 41, p. 237).
5. **[book]** Show corrective feedback *after* the whole quiz, not per item (p. 40).
6. **[book → SM-2]** After the post-class quiz, the first SM-2 review should be no sooner
   than the next day; that matches SM-2's 1-day first interval and the book's "at least a day
   in between sessions" (p. 63-64).
7. **[book → calibration]** Do **not** treat a high post-class score as mastery: ease of
   retrieval "on a quiz shortly after encountering it in a lecture or text" is not a reliable
   cue, while "Ease of retrieval after a delay ... is a good indicator" (p. 126). Feed
   calibration stats from delayed reviews, not same-day ones.

## 3. Spacing across days and the semester

- **[book]** Interval rule of thumb: "enough so that practice doesn't become a mindless
  repetition. At a minimum, enough time so that a little forgetting has set in", but not so
  much "that retrieval essentially involves relearning" (p. 63). Sleep matters, "so practice
  with at least a day in between sessions is good" (p. 63-64).
- **[book]** A concrete ladder for text material: revisit "within a day or so", then "not
  again for several days or a week", then "once a month" once you feel sure (p. 204).
- **[book]** Never retire important items: keep correct flashcards in the deck "until they're
  well mastered", then revisit "perhaps monthly" (p. 204); "Don't make the mistake of
  dropping material from your testing regime once you've gotten it correct a couple of times"
  (p. 125). Students drop cards "far sooner than they should" (p. 124). Leitner box: "the
  better your mastery, the less frequent the practice, but ... it will never disappear" (p. 64).
- **[book]** Make it cumulative: "Set aside a little time every week throughout the semester
  to quiz yourself ... both the current week's work and material covered in prior weeks"
  (p. 202); reach back and ask how old knowledge "relates to what you have subsequently
  learned" (p. 204). Cumulative quizzing is "especially powerful" (p. 125; Sobel's nine
  quizzes roll early material forward, p. 64).

**Rules for Articulate**
8. **[book → SM-2]** SM-2's growing intervals fit the book, but cap the maximum interval
   around a month for active-course material (p. 204), and never auto-suspend a card for being
   answered right twice (p. 124-125).
9. **[book]** Every daily quiz should include some old material from earlier in each course,
   not only today's lecture (p. 202, p. 227).

## 4. Interleaving across courses and topics

- **[book]** Interleaving is a form of spacing: alternating topics "requires that you
  continually refresh your mind on each topic as you return to it" (p. 204; p. 65). "You
  switch before each practice is complete" (p. 65).
- **[book]** Effect size and its cost: blocked practice scored 89% vs 60% during practice, but
  20% vs 63% a week later (p. 49-50). It feels slower and learners keep believing blocked is
  better (p. 47, p. 50, p. 207).
- **[book]** Timing: interleave once "you understand a new problem type and its solution but
  your grasp of it is still rudimentary" (p. 206). Its main payoff is learning to tell problem
  types apart and pick the right solution (p. 206-207, p. 65).
- **[book]** Shuffle: "You need to shuffle your flashcards" (p. 66).

**Rules for Articulate**
10. **[book]** Mix the daily quiz across courses and topics; never serve one course as a
    block. Shuffle order every time (p. 66, p. 204).
11. **[inference]** Inside one course, mix problem types the student has already met at
    least once; keep brand-new types out of the interleaved mix until after the first
    post-class retrieval (p. 206).
12. **[inference]** Interleaving *related* problem types in one course (which formula
    applies?) gives the discrimination benefit (p. 206-207); mixing *unrelated* courses gives
    mainly the spacing benefit (p. 204). Both are worth doing; the first is the one the
    book's strongest data is about.

## 5. Question format

- **[book]** Supply-the-answer formats (short answer, essay, flashcards) "appear to be more
  effective than simple recognition tests like multiple choice" (p. 40-41); "Having to write a
  short essay makes them stronger still" (p. 87). Multiple choice still helps (p. 41).
- **[book]** Effortful retrieval is the point: "The greater the effort to retrieve learning,
  provided that you succeed, the more that learning is strengthened" (p. 43; also p. 100).

**Rule 13. [book]** Default to free recall, explain-it and short-answer; use multiple choice
sparingly. This fits Articulate's voice answers.

## 6. Calibration

- **[book]** Calibration means "aligning your judgments of what you know and don't know with
  objective feedback" (p. 210). Actually answer: don't say "Yup, I know that" and move on;
  "Treat practice tests as tests, check your answers" (p. 210-211).
- **[book]** Feelings of fluency mislead; rereaders are overconfident, and quizzed students get
  "a more accurate sense of what they know and don't know" (p. 42; p. 202). Learners who
  benefit from spacing/interleaving "don't perceive the improvement" (p. 47).
- **[book]** The fix is "objective gauges outside ourselves" (p. 124); explaining a text in
  your own words is "an excellent cue for judging comprehension" (p. 126). Use quizzing "to
  identify areas of weak mastery, and focus your studying to make them strong" (p. 202).

**Rules for Articulate**
14. **[book]** Keep PLAN.md's confidence-before-answer, but require an actual answer every
    time; never offer "I know this, skip" (p. 210).
15. **[book]** Show the student, over time, where confidence and score diverge, and steer extra
    items toward high-confidence misses (p. 202, p. 211).
16. **[inference]** Warn the user up front (as Wenderoth does with "transparency", p. 229) that
    spaced, mixed quizzes will feel harder and less productive; that is expected (p. 47,
    p. 205).

## 7. Session length

The book gives **no minute target** for a self-study session. What it does give:

- **[book]** Classroom quizzes "took only a few minutes" (p. 34); McDermott's is 3-5 minutes
  (p. 237); Wenderoth's free recall is 10 minutes (p. 231).
- **[book]** In a 1917 study, the best results came from spending "about 60 percent of the
  study time in recitation" rather than rereading (p. 30).
- **[book]** More spaced sessions beat fewer long ones: "Lots of practice works, but only if
  it's spaced" (p. 205); multiple retrieval sessions are better than one, "especially if the
  test sessions are spaced out" (p. 32). The surgeons' four lessons spread a week apart beat
  four lessons in one day (p. 48).

**Rule 17. [inference]** Keep each sitting short (the book's examples run 3-10 minutes) and
spread retrieval across the day (preview, post-class, daily review) rather than one long block.
The exact daily length is a product decision the book does not settle.

## 8. How exam proximity should change the mix

The book does not give a ramp schedule. Its guidance:

- **[book]** Regular spaced retrieval all term "puts an end to cramming and all-nighters. You
  will need little studying at exam time. Reviewing the material the night before is much
  easier than learning it" (p. 203). Frequently tested students "did not need to cram"
  (p. 42).
- **[book]** Cramming wins the next day and loses after: crammers forgot 50% within two days vs
  13% for retrieval practice (p. 31); it "may get you through the next day's midterm, but most
  of the material will be long forgotten by the time you sit down for the final" (p. 48;
  "binge-and-purge", p. 63).
- **[book]** The tested model before an exam: a review quiz "twenty-four hours before each
  unit exam" (p. 34). A student preparing for the MCAT "took a practice test every three days,
  saw what I got wrong, and adjusted" (p. 236). Cumulative exams force spaced review (p. 238).

**Rules for Articulate**
18. **[book]** Never switch to cramming. The daily spaced/interleaved quiz keeps running up to
    the exam, for every course, including courses with no exam soon (p. 31, p. 48, p. 203).
19. **[inference from p. 34, p. 236]** As an exam nears (say the last ~10 days), *raise the
    share* of that course in the daily mix and add full practice tests every few days that
    sample the whole exam scope, with weak items fed back into SM-2. Do not pull other
    courses out.
20. **[book]** Day before the exam: one cumulative review quiz over the exam scope, with
    feedback (p. 34, p. 203). No new material.
21. **[book]** After an exam, keep the material in rotation at long intervals; the Columbia
    effect would have been bigger with retrieval "once a month, say, in the intervening
    months" (p. 35), and finals are often cumulative (p. 238).

## 9. Weekly layer (fits PLAN.md §10-11)

- **[book]** Weekly summary sheet: one page drawing the week's material and how the parts
  interrelate (p. 231, p. 208).
- **[book]** Weekly "learning paragraph": a five- or six-sentence answer to a question like "How
  is the GI tract like the respiratory system?" or "what would you do differently next time?"
  (p. 232; reflection, p. 209-210).

These map well onto the existing Weekly Synthesis and Weekly ZK Quiz.

## Proposed daily rhythm (synthesis)

| When | What | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Before each class (optional) | 2-3 generation prompts: predict key ideas; try 1-2 problems | p. 209, p. 227, p. 88; must be answerable, p. 98-99 |
| Right after class | 3-5 item closed-book quiz on that lecture, free recall/short answer, feedback at end; preview answers corrected here | p. 34, p. 237, p. 40-41, p. 101 |
| End of day (optional) | 10-minute free recall of the day's classes, then check notes | p. 231 |
| Daily review (MieracleStudy) | SM-2 due items, interleaved across courses, shuffled, includes older material; first review of today's lecture no sooner than tomorrow | p. 63-64, p. 202-206, p. 43 |
| Exam within ~10 days | Course weight goes up; practice test every ~3 days | p. 236 (inference on timing) |
| Day before exam | One cumulative review quiz with feedback | p. 34, p. 203 |
| Weekly | Summary sheet / learning paragraph; cumulative quiz | p. 231-232, p. 125 |

## Open questions for Amier

1. **Preview source:** syllabus topic plus assigned reading, or a transcript of the *previous*
   lecture? The book only backs generation on material you can reason about (p. 98-99).
2. **Post-class quiz timing:** Canvas gives class times; should the quiz fire at class end, or
   when the transcript is ready (could be hours later)? The book's evidence is for right at the
   end of class (p. 34, p. 237).
3. **Session budget:** what is a realistic daily total (e.g. 15 min? 30 min?)? The book gives no
   number; this decides how many SM-2 items fit.
4. **Exam ramp:** is "last ~10 days, practice test every ~3 days" right for your courses, or
   do you want it tied to exam weight?
5. **Interval cap:** cap SM-2 intervals near a month for active courses (p. 204), or trust SM-2's
   own growth?
6. **Books and learning goals:** should they share the same interleaved daily quiz with
   courses, or get their own slot so exam pressure does not crowd them out?
